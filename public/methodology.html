Data and Methodology

Each flash on the globe represents one real human death, timed and placed from public demographic data; the feed at the bottom gives each one a statistically representative identity — an age, a sex and a cause drawn from real distributions. No flash or persona corresponds to a specific identifiable person: every event is statistical, not a record of an individual.

How often people die (the points)

Each country emits deaths as an independent Poisson process. A country's deaths per year are computed as

deaths/year = crude death rate × population ÷ 1000

That annual total sets the mean interval between flashes (one year ÷ deaths-per-year). Each gap between consecutive deaths is then drawn from an exponential distribution with that mean, so deaths arrive irregularly — clustering and spacing out — rather than on a fixed beat, while the long-run count still matches the country's real annual total. Summed over every country this is roughly two deaths every second worldwide.

  • Crude death rate & population — World Bank Open Data (indicators SP.DYN.CDRT.IN and SP.POP.TOTL), most recent year available per country. The World Bank's crude death rate is itself derived from the UN World Population Prospects.

Where deaths appear (placement)

Within a country, a death is not dropped at a random point but sampled from a population-density grid, so flashes concentrate where people actually live and each country's real annual total is preserved.

  • Population density — Gridded Population of the World, v4 (GPWv4), aggregated to a 0.5° grid. Each cell's population sets how likely a death is to land there: a country's annual deaths are split across its cells in proportion to the people living in each, so dense regions flash far more often than empty ones.

Who each person is (the personas)

When a flash fires we generate one short persona for the feed, e.g. "Woman 84, lung cancer – Japan". Because the dot is a synthetic Poisson event there is no real person behind it, so we fabricate an identity that is statistically representative of who actually dies in that country. It is built in three steps, each from real, sex- and age-specific data.

Deaths are binned into nine age bands — <1, 1–4, 5–14, 15–29, 30–49, 50–64, 65–74, 75–84, 85+ — used consistently for sex, age and cause.

  1. Sex. We take the country's total male vs. female deaths and pick the decedent's sex in that proportion — so a flash inherits the real sex balance of mortality where it fired.
  2. Age. Given that sex, we pick an age band with probability proportional to the real number of deaths in each band for that country and sex, then choose a uniform age within the band. This is why a death in Japan or Spain almost always reads as elderly while one in Nigeria is far more likely to be an infant or child — it reflects each country's actual age-at-death curve, not a global average.
  3. Cause. Given the sex and age band, we draw a cause of death from a sex- and age-stratified cause table: only causes plausible for that sex and age are eligible (e.g. prostate cancer for older men, breast cancer for women, neonatal complications only for infants, road injuries and suicide weighted toward young adults, ischaemic heart disease, stroke and dementia toward the old), each with a weight reflecting how common it is in that band. A cause is then chosen at random in proportion to those weights.
  • Age & sex of deaths — UN World Population Prospects, Data Portal API, indicator "Deaths by 5-year age groups and sex" (median variant, most recent year, ~170 countries). The absolute death counts per age group and sex are the sampling weights for steps 1 and 2 above, re-binned into the nine bands.
  • Cause of death — a compact, sex- and age-band-stratified table of causes informed by the Global Burden of Disease and WHO Global Health Estimates cause-of-death patterns. The eligibility ranges and weights are accurate by sex and age band; they are applied globally rather than re-estimated per country, so the cause matches the persona's sex and age, but not the country's specific epidemiological profile.

Identities are representative, never real: the same age, sex and cause distributions could describe thousands of people, and no line is tied to an actual individual.

What you see (visualization)

Each death is rendered as an "atomic blast seen from space":

  • A double flash — a near-instant first pulse, a brief dark minimum, then a longer second pulse that fades — echoing the double-flash signature of a nuclear detonation (the real first pulse lasts about a millisecond; here it is slowed so it is visible).
  • A single, subtle shockwave that refracts the surface like one expanding water ripple, then dissipates.

Centring on you

On load the globe rotates to centre on your approximate location, looked up from your IP address via the free service ip-api.com. Only coordinates and a place name are used, cached briefly in memory and never stored. If the lookup is unavailable the globe simply keeps its default view.

Caveats

This is an illustrative visualization, not a surveillance or forecasting tool. Rates are national averages applied uniformly in time; density placement is approximate; and the blast imagery is a metaphor, not a depiction of cause or manner of death. Personas are statistically representative, not real people — age and sex are real per-country distributions, but the cause is drawn from a global sex/age table, so it fits the persona's sex and age rather than the country's specific disease mix. Figures reflect the latest available data, which lags real time by one or more years.

Built with three.js. Source data: World Bank Open Data, UN World Population Prospects (Data Portal API), CIESIN GPWv4, with cause-of-death patterns informed by IHME GBD and WHO.
